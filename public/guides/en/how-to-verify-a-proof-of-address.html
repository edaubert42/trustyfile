How to verify a proof of address

Updated 11 November 2026

Proof of address is among the most forged documents, because it is requested everywhere and verified almost nowhere. In most cases it is a genuine bill belonging to somebody else, with the name and address swapped.

In short

  • A fake proof of address is usually someone else's genuine bill with the name and address replaced.
  • A utility or telecom bill produced by a word processor, an online converter or a screenshot tool contradicts the provider it claims to come from.
  • Pasting leaves measurable traces: a resolution break, a white that differs from the background, noise or compression concentrated on one block.
  • A scanned or photographed proof of address cannot be verified technically: ask for the original PDF.

The dominant scenario: name substitution

Unlike a fraudulent invoice, a forged proof of address rarely starts from scratch. The forger takes a real bill — their own, or one found online — and replaces only the fields they care about: name and address.

That very localised edit is what makes it detectable: the rest of the document is genuine, so every inconsistency concentrates on the edited area.

Compare the producing software with the claimed issuer

Large utilities and telecom operators generate bills through industrial pipelines with very stable technical fingerprints: same PDF producer, same embedded fonts, same internal structure month after month.

A document claiming to come from a major supplier whose producer is an office suite, an online converter or a screenshot tool contradicts what it claims to be.

Look for paste artifacts

When the name is replaced with an image rather than text, the paste leaves measurable traces.

  • Resolution break: a 96 dpi block sitting on a 300 dpi page.
  • White that is not the same white: a document background is rarely pure white, a pasted rectangle often is.
  • Inconsistent local noise: a scan has uniform noise across the page; a re-pasted region is abnormally smooth or abnormally noisy.
  • JPEG compression artifacts concentrated around one block of text.

The scanned document: knowing when to say "I don't know"

A document scanned and flattened into an image has no text layer, no fonts and no revision history left. Nearly every check described here becomes inapplicable.

This matters methodologically: the absence of anomalies means nothing when there was nothing to examine. TrustyFile therefore reports such documents as unverifiable rather than giving them a good score by default. Many tools do the opposite, which is misleading.

Asking for the digital original instead of a scan or a photo turns an impossible verification into a possible one. It is the single best move when you are in a position to insist.

Non-technical consistency checks

  • Is the bill date inside the required window, often three months?
  • Is the consumption period earlier than the issue date? A bill charging for the future is an anomaly.
  • Does the customer number match the supplier's format?
  • Is the amount plausible for the property and the season?

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a proof of address is fake?
Compare the software that produced the PDF with the claimed provider, look for paste artifacts around the name and address, and check consistency: issue date within the required window, billing period before the issue date, customer number in the provider's format.
Can a scanned proof of address be verified?
Not technically. A flattened scan has no text layer, no fonts and no history left, so finding no anomaly means nothing. TrustyFile marks it unverifiable rather than giving it a good score.
What should I ask for to be able to verify a proof of address?
The original PDF, downloaded from the provider's customer account, rather than a scan or a photo.
